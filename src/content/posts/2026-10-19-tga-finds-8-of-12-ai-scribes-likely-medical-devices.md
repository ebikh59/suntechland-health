## What happened

The Therapeutic Goods Administration has found that eight of 12 digital scribe products it examined include functions likely to meet the definition of a medical device, even though none of their suppliers identified their products as medical devices. Two further products required additional information before their status could be determined. The TGA published the outcome of its review on 16 October 2026.

The reviewed products included scribes from Heidi, Lyrebird Health, Microsoft, Abby Health, Carepatron and iMedX. However, the TGA did not identify which products had the functions in question. A spokesperson said follow-up with suppliers is continuing. Critics have said the lack of product-level detail leaves clinicians without clear information.

The regulator has strengthened its guidance for suppliers. It now states that a digital scribe is not a medical device when its sole purpose is to record, transcribe and convert a clinical conversation into structured text records. Functions that go further may bring a product within regulation. The TGA also said that statements or disclaimers are not enough to determine whether a product is a medical device. It has set out four compliance pathways for scribes with regulated functions.

The TGA estimated there were about 100 digital scribe suppliers in Australia in 2025, with existing products adding new features and new products entering the market during 2026.

## Technical and operational significance

The line the TGA is drawing is about function, not marketing. A scribe that transcribes and structures a consultation is a documentation tool. A scribe that suggests diagnoses, recommends tests or treatments, or generates clinical conclusions is doing something different, and errors in those outputs could directly affect care.

Feature creep is the practical risk. Products that began as transcription tools are adding summarisation, coding, referral letters, recommendations and agent-like capabilities. Each addition may change a product's regulatory status, sometimes without users noticing.

For security and privacy, scribes are already significant. They capture full audio and transcripts of consultations, often process them in the cloud, and integrate with clinical systems. Regulation as a medical device would add requirements around quality management and post-market monitoring, but would not replace privacy and security due diligence.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

General practices, specialist rooms and hospitals using AI scribes should not assume a product is outside TGA regulation because its supplier says so. The TGA has explicitly said disclaimers are not determinative.

Health organisations should understand which features of their scribe they actually use, and whether any go beyond transcription. If they do, they should ask the supplier about its regulatory status and plans for compliance. Clinicians also remain responsible for reviewing and approving any scribe-generated content before it enters the record.

## What to watch next

- Whether the TGA names products or publishes compliance actions following its supplier follow-up.
- Suppliers changing features or seeking inclusion in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods.
- The TGA's review of clinical decision support guidance and related amendments.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Inventory AI scribe products in use and document which features are enabled, such as transcription only, summaries, recommendations or coding.
- Ask suppliers in writing about their TGA regulatory assessment and compliance pathway, especially for features that go beyond transcription.
- Disable scribe features that generate clinical recommendations unless their regulatory status and safety have been confirmed.
- Complete a privacy and security assessment covering audio and transcript storage, data residency, retention and any use of data for model training.
- Keep clinician review and sign-off mandatory for all scribe-generated documentation.
