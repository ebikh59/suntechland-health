## What happened

The Therapeutic Goods Administration is considering changes to how digital mental health tools are regulated. A review found that some higher-risk products, including those using artificial intelligence, could be exempt from medical device oversight under the current rules, according to Pulse+IT.

Digital mental health tools are currently excluded from the definition of a medical device and are not regulated by the TGA as therapeutic goods, provided they meet the exclusion's conditions. Software that only provides low-risk functions and does not diagnose, monitor or treat a condition falls outside regulation. The review found that the existing exclusion criteria may no longer be fit for purpose as digital mental health products evolve.

The TGA's earlier review of AI regulation had concluded that the exclusion for digital mental health tools was not appropriate and recommended an urgent review of how these tools are regulated. The regulator has since sought input from stakeholders and users, held an information session on reviewing the exclusion, and indicated that a consultation on regulatory options would inform any changes.

## Technical and operational significance

Digital mental health is one of the fastest-moving areas of health software. Tools range from simple self-help and mood tracking apps to AI chatbots that hold open-ended conversations with people in distress, assess risk, and suggest actions. An exclusion designed for low-risk wellbeing apps may not suit products whose outputs could influence decisions by vulnerable users.

AI-enabled tools raise particular concerns:

- **Unpredictable outputs.** Generative AI can produce inappropriate or harmful responses, especially in crisis situations.
- **Risk assessment without oversight.** Tools that triage or assess risk may effectively perform clinical functions.
- **Sensitive data.** Mental health conversations are among the most sensitive health information, and many apps are offered directly to consumers by companies outside the traditional health sector.

Bringing higher-risk tools under medical device regulation would introduce requirements for evidence, quality management and post-market monitoring. Privacy and security obligations under the Privacy Act would continue to apply regardless.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Health services, primary health networks and clinicians who recommend or commission digital mental health tools should not assume that a tool's current unregulated status means it is safe. Until the regulatory position changes, due diligence falls largely on those selecting and recommending the tools.

That due diligence should cover clinical evidence and safety features, such as escalation to human support and crisis response, as well as how the tool handles data: where conversations are stored, who can access them, whether they are used for training AI models, and whether data is shared with third parties for advertising or analytics.

## What to watch next

- Publication of the TGA's consultation paper and proposed options for regulating digital mental health tools.
- Any revised exclusion criteria, and transition arrangements for existing products.
- Related regulatory changes for AI-enabled software more broadly.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Maintain a register of digital mental health tools your organisation commissions, recommends or integrates, including whether they use AI.
- Assess each tool's clinical evidence, crisis escalation pathways and safeguards against harmful outputs before recommending it.
- Review the privacy practices of digital mental health tools, including data storage location, retention, third-party sharing and use of conversations for AI training.
- Contribute to the TGA consultation, sharing your organisation's experience of benefits and risks.
- Prepare for possible regulatory change by asking suppliers how they would comply if their products became regulated medical devices.
