## What happened

The US Department of Homeland Security and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) have announced a new framework for public-private collaboration on critical infrastructure security, the Alliance of National Councils for Homeland Operational Resilience–Critical Infrastructure (ANCHOR-CI).

ANCHOR-CI replaces the Critical Infrastructure Partnership Advisory Council (CIPAC), which DHS established in March 2006 and which was eliminated in March 2025 by then-Secretary Kristi Noem, according to HIPAA Journal. The new framework will run for two years initially and can be extended by the DHS Secretary under Section 871 of the Homeland Security Act.

ANCHOR-CI will operate through four types of councils: sector, cross-sector, industry and regional coordinating councils. CISA approves members and may appoint more. Like its predecessor, the framework is exempt from the Federal Advisory Committee Act and keeps CIPAC's protections, but liability protection for participants has been dropped.

For the healthcare and public health sector, the HHS Office of Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Protection will work with DHS and CISA to elevate the sector's priorities.

"The new and innovative ANCHOR-CI framework will be a game changer in how the public and private sectors collaborate," said DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin.

The announcement did not give the number of members or councils, or when councils will begin meeting.

## Technical and operational significance

Formal coordination structures matter because they create a protected space where companies and government can share threat information, vulnerabilities and incident details that they would not otherwise disclose publicly. The FACA exemption allows these discussions to happen without the openness requirements that apply to other federal advisory bodies.

The gap between CIPAC's elimination in March 2025 and ANCHOR-CI's launch disrupted some of that collaboration. How quickly the new councils become active, and whether sectors trust them enough to resume sharing, will determine their value.

The removal of liability protection for participants is worth watching. Organisations may be more cautious about what they share if they fear legal exposure.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

The framework is US-specific, but it affects Australian health organisations indirectly. Much of the threat intelligence used by Australian health services originates from US government and industry sharing, including through Health-ISAC and CISA advisories. Stronger US coordination on healthcare threats tends to produce better and faster intelligence that Australian defenders can use.

It is also a useful comparison point for Australia's own arrangements. Australian health organisations can join the ACSC's partnership programmes, and hospitals covered by the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act already have reporting obligations. Health services that are not yet active in trusted sharing networks miss out on early warnings that peers receive.

## What to watch next

- Formation of the healthcare and public health sector council under ANCHOR-CI and its early activity.
- Whether the removal of liability protection affects industry participation.
- Changes in the volume and timeliness of US healthcare threat advisories.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Join the ACSC Partnership Program and relevant industry sharing groups, such as Health-ISAC, to receive early threat intelligence.
- Subscribe to CISA, ACSC and Health-ISAC advisories, and assign responsibility for triaging and acting on them.
- Establish internal processes and legal guidance for sharing threat information with trusted partners, so staff know what can be shared and how.
- Contribute anonymised incident and threat information back to sharing communities, strengthening the sector's collective defence.
- Review obligations under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act if your organisation operates a critical hospital.
