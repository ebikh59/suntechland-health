## What happened
The Royal Australasian College of Physicians (RACP) has publicly highlighted a growing concern in Australian health services: clinicians are increasingly expected to rely on artificial intelligence (AI) tools without sufficient organisational safeguards. In its recent opinion piece, the RACP describes this as a "real and urgent problem" and stresses that the solution lies in robust governance – specifically, ensuring the right mix of expertise is present when AI systems are selected, deployed and monitored. The college’s warning is a call to action for health‑care leaders to recognise that AI, while promising, carries blind spots that can jeopardise patient safety if left unchecked.

## Technical and operational significance
AI applications in health‑care—ranging from diagnostic image analysis to predictive risk scores—are built on complex machine‑learning models that learn from historical data. These models can inherit biases, misinterpret rare conditions, or fail when presented with data that differ from the training set. The RACP’s commentary underscores that the technical risk is not merely a matter of algorithmic error; it is amplified when clinicians are the sole custodians of these tools.

From an operational perspective, the lack of a formal governance framework means:
- **Decision‑making is siloed.** Clinicians may adopt AI outputs without input from data scientists, ethicists or risk managers, leading to unchecked reliance on opaque recommendations.
- **Model drift goes unnoticed.** Without systematic performance monitoring, an AI system that once performed well can degrade over time as patient populations or clinical practices evolve.
- **Accountability is unclear.** When an AI‑driven error occurs, it can be difficult to determine whether responsibility lies with the clinician, the vendor, or the institution.

These issues align with recognised cyber‑security and privacy concerns in the Australian context, such as the need for continuous monitoring (ASD Essential Eight “Patch Applications”) and the principle of least privilege (controlling who can modify or access AI models). While the RACP piece does not detail a specific breach or incident, the technical implications are clear: without governance, AI blind spots become a systemic vulnerability.

## What it means for Australian healthcare
For CISOs, IT leaders, privacy officers and clinical‑technology teams, the RACP’s warning translates into a practical mandate to embed AI governance into existing risk‑management processes. The Australian Government’s **Security Legislation Amendment (Critical Infrastructure) Act 2021** and the **Privacy Act 1988 (Australian Privacy Principles)** provide a regulatory backdrop that encourages organisations to assess the impact of new technologies on data security and patient privacy.

Key take‑aways for Australian health services include:
1. **Multidisciplinary oversight is essential.** Governance bodies should include clinicians, data scientists, information security specialists and legal counsel to evaluate AI tools before deployment.
2. **Risk assessments must be ongoing.** AI systems should be subject to the same continuous monitoring required for traditional IT assets, including performance audits and bias checks.
3. **Transparency and explainability matter.** Clinicians need clear documentation on how an AI model reaches its conclusions, supporting informed clinical judgement and facilitating audit trails.
4. **Training and awareness are non‑negotiable.** Staff must understand both the capabilities and limitations of AI, reducing the likelihood of over‑reliance.
5. **Incident response plans should incorporate AI failures.** Existing cyber‑incident frameworks need to be extended to cover algorithmic errors that could affect patient outcomes.

By treating AI governance as a component of the broader cyber‑risk landscape, health organisations can better align with the **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC)** guidance on risk management and the **Essential Eight** mitigation strategies, particularly those related to application whitelisting, patching, and privileged access management.

## What to watch next
The RACP’s call for governance is likely to spur several developments in the Australian health sector:
- **Formal policy frameworks.** State and territory health departments may issue directives mandating AI governance committees, mirroring the approach taken by other regulated industries.
- **Regulatory clarification.** The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) could publish guidance on the privacy implications of AI‑driven clinical decision support, especially concerning the handling of sensitive health data.
- **Industry standards.** Professional bodies such as the Australian Digital Health Agency may develop technical standards for model validation, version control and audit logging.
- **Vendor accountability.** Procurement contracts may increasingly require AI vendors to provide evidence of bias testing, model explainability and post‑deployment support.
- **Research and benchmarking.** Academic collaborations could produce Australian‑specific datasets for benchmarking AI performance, helping to mitigate the risk of models trained on non‑representative data.

Given the rapid pace of AI adoption, health‑care leaders should monitor announcements from the RACP, the ACSC and relevant state health ministries for updates on mandatory governance requirements. Until formal guidance is published, the prudent approach is to adopt the principles outlined above, treating AI governance as an integral part of the organisation’s cyber‑security and risk‑management strategy.

*Note: The article draws exclusively on the RACP’s opinion piece, which flags the problem and proposes governance as the remedy. No specific incidents, dates, CVEs or regulatory findings were cited in the source material, and the analysis reflects the limited information available.*

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Establish a cross‑functional AI governance committee that includes clinicians, data scientists, information security staff and legal counsel to review and approve all AI tools before clinical use.
- Implement continuous performance monitoring for deployed AI models, including regular bias assessments and drift detection, aligned with the ASD Essential Eight practice of patching and updating applications.
- Document model decision‑making processes and provide explainability summaries to clinicians, ensuring transparency and supporting audit trails for any adverse outcomes.
- Deliver targeted training for clinical and technical staff on AI limitations, safe usage practices and the importance of questioning AI outputs, fulfilling ACSC guidance on security awareness.
- Integrate AI‑related failure scenarios into the organisation’s incident response plan, defining clear escalation paths and remediation steps for algorithmic errors that could impact patient safety.
