## What happened

The North Los Angeles County Regional Center (NLACRC), which coordinates services for people with developmental disabilities, is mailing notification letters about a ransomware attack that took place in late 2024.

According to HIPAA Journal, unauthorised access occurred between 20 November and 1 December 2024. The incident was first identified on 28 November 2024 and announced on the centre's website on 6 January 2025. Data was exfiltrated before files were encrypted. The Medusa ransomware group claimed responsibility and alleged it stole more than 600GB.

The exposed information is extensive: Social Security numbers, passport and driver's licence numbers, financial and payment card information, health plan details, medical record numbers, laboratory results, medication, diagnosis and treatment information, and disability codes, among other data.

The HHS Office for Civil Rights lists 500 affected individuals, but HIPAA Journal said: "That is a placeholder figure, as the breach was reported to OCR on January 6, 2025." The true number is likely much higher.

The same report covered **Midland Care Connection** in Topeka, Kansas, which detected suspicious activity on 31 March 2026. Unauthorised access had begun on 30 March. Its data review, completed on 12 June 2026, found names, dates of birth, medical and health insurance information, financial account information and, for some people, Social Security numbers. It is offering 12 months of credit monitoring. The number affected is not yet known.

## Technical and operational significance

The NLACRC case highlights the gap between public announcement and individual notification. The incident was announced within about six weeks, but individual letters are only now being sent, around 19 months after the attack. The people whose data was stolen, many of them people with disabilities and their families, may not have known they were affected in the meantime.

Medusa uses double extortion: stealing data before encrypting systems. That means restoring from backup does not end the threat, and stolen data may be published regardless of recovery.

Disability codes and service information are particularly sensitive, and their exposure can lead to discrimination or targeting of vulnerable people.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Australian disability service providers, NDIS-registered organisations and community health services hold similar combinations of identity, financial, health and disability information about people who may be particularly vulnerable to fraud and exploitation.

These organisations should plan for breach notification that is accessible: easy-to-read formats, support for carers and guardians, and channels beyond letters and email. Under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, notification should happen as soon as practicable after assessment, not many months later.

Reducing the range of sensitive data held, such as payment card details and copies of identity documents, also limits the impact of an attack.

## What to watch next

- The final number of people affected by the NLACRC breach.
- Whether Medusa has published the stolen data.
- The count and cause of the Midland Care Connection incident.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Plan accessible breach notification for people with disability, including easy-read materials and engagement with carers, guardians and advocates.
- Minimise collection and retention of identity documents, payment card data and other information not essential for service delivery.
- Monitor for data exfiltration as well as encryption, since double-extortion groups steal data before deploying ransomware.
- Set internal targets for completing breach assessments and notifying individuals within Notifiable Data Breaches expectations.
- Include disability and community service providers in sector threat-sharing, and apply the ASD Essential Eight as a baseline.
