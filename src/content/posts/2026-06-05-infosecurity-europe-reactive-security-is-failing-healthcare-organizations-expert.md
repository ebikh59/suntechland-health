## What happened

Healthcare organisations must move from reactive to proactive security or keep risking potentially serious consequences for patients, experts warned at Infosecurity Europe on 4 June.

Sher Baig, CEO of Cyber Salus, told attendees that healthcare organisations around the world face the same threats and constraints. He said legacy infrastructure, hyper-connectivity and human fatigue are creating "a perfect storm of risk", and that in rare cases breaches can contribute to patient deaths. "If there was ever an industry where the potential harm bad actors can do is directly correlated to human impact, it's healthcare," he said.

The talk cited Proofpoint research finding that 93% of healthcare organisations suffered at least one cyber-attack in 2025, averaging 43 attacks per organisation, up from 40 in 2024.

Baig singled out connected devices such as infusion pumps, imaging systems, patient monitors and laboratory systems. "These devices are in the field for 15 to 20 years running legacy operating systems," he said.

## Technical and operational significance

Baig argued that the traditional model of discovering a vulnerability after exposure, then scrambling to assess and contain it, is becoming unsustainable as AI shortens the time between disclosure and exploitation. Alert overload and slow manual investigations compound the problem. AI is also making phishing more effective.

He set out four steps for a more proactive posture:

- Gain full visibility of devices and threats, down to device-level details such as software versions.
- Prioritise threats by clinical risk, so issues affecting devices critical to patient care are addressed first.
- Use AI to correlate signals and reduce alert fatigue in security operations.
- Patch where possible, segment to reduce exposure, and use AI to select the most suitable compensating control.

Other experts added important caveats. Rob Demain, CEO of e2e-assure, agreed that "reactive to predictive is the right direction for healthcare" but warned that "predictive is not a product you switch on". He said most healthcare organisations lack clean, complete data, much of their equipment cannot run an agent or be patched, and large parts of the network are invisible. "No model predicts what it cannot see. The honest first move is not predictive AI, it is basic coverage of the estate," he said.

Chris Newton-Smith, CEO of IO, said AI is amplifying existing weaknesses rather than creating new ones, and "cannot compensate for fragmented processes, weak governance or overstretched teams".

## What it means for Australian healthcare

The discussion was about healthcare generally, but its themes describe Australian hospitals well. These include long-lived clinical devices, flat or partly segmented networks, stretched security teams and a growing number of connected systems.

The most practical takeaway is the sequence. Visibility comes first, then prioritisation by clinical impact, then automation. An Australian health service that does not yet have an accurate inventory of its connected medical devices will get little value from predictive or AI-driven tools.

Prioritising by clinical risk is also a useful bridge between security and clinical governance. Framing a vulnerable infusion pump or imaging modality in terms of patient-safety impact helps clinical leaders weigh security work against service delivery.

## What to watch next

- Adoption of AI-assisted security operations in health services, and evidence of whether it reduces alert fatigue and response times.
- Progress on medical device inventories and segmentation projects across Australian hospital networks.
- Continued attack volumes against healthcare reported in vendor and government threat reporting.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Build a complete asset inventory covering IT, clinical devices and IoT, including software versions, network location and the owning clinical service.
- Add a clinical-impact rating to vulnerability management so that issues affecting critical care devices are fixed or mitigated first.
- Segment medical devices and building systems from corporate networks, and record the compensating controls for each device that cannot be patched.
- Before buying AI-driven detection tools, confirm the telemetry they need, such as network flow data, device logs and identity events, is actually collected across the estate.
- Report security metrics to executive and clinical governance committees in terms of patient-safety and service-continuity risk.
