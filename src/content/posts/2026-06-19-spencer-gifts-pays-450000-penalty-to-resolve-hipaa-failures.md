## What happened

The US Department of Health and Human Services' Office for Civil Rights (OCR) has settled alleged HIPAA violations with Spencer Gifts LLC and its employer-sponsored group health plan, the Spencer Gifts LLC Flexible Benefits and Welfare Benefit Plans.

The case stems from a ransomware attack in which a threat actor accessed the company's network between 24 and 26 November 2021 and encrypted files on servers holding the health plan's electronic protected health information. OCR was notified on 24 January 2022. The breach affected 10,023 individuals and involved names, addresses, ZIP codes, phone numbers, email addresses and Social Security numbers. The attacker has not been named.

OCR's investigation found that the plan had not conducted a compliant risk analysis, in violation of 45 C.F.R. § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A), and had failed to implement required policies and procedures (§§ 164.316(a) and 164.530(i)(1)). The findings are described as alleged violations.

The settlement includes a US$450,000 payment and a corrective action plan requiring a comprehensive risk analysis, updated and distributed policies and procedures, and HIPAA training.

"Effective cybersecurity starts with Security Rule compliance," said OCR Director Paula M. Stannard.

## Technical and operational significance

The case is a reminder that HIPAA obligations apply to employer-sponsored group health plans, not just hospitals and insurers. A retailer's benefits plan held health and identity data on more than 10,000 people, and that data sat on servers vulnerable to ransomware.

It also reflects OCR's consistent enforcement focus. According to HIPAA Journal, this is OCR's 20th ransomware-related penalty, its 14th enforcement action under its risk analysis initiative, and its seventh HIPAA penalty of the year, with US$1,728,000 collected so far. The repeated finding is the same: organisations that suffer ransomware often have not completed an accurate, organisation-wide risk analysis that identifies where sensitive data is held and what threats it faces.

The time between the incident (late 2021) and the settlement (mid-2026) shows that regulatory consequences can arrive years after a breach.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Australia does not have an equivalent to HIPAA, but the underlying lesson applies. The Privacy Act requires organisations to take reasonable steps to protect personal information, and the OAIC has emphasised that knowing what data you hold and assessing risks to it is the foundation of reasonable steps. Recent OAIC actions following major breaches have focused on whether organisations understood and managed their security risks.

For Australian employers, health funds, and organisations administering health-related benefits or workers' compensation, the case is a prompt to ensure health information held outside clinical settings is included in risk assessments and security programmes.

A documented, current risk assessment is also one of the first things regulators, insurers and auditors will ask for after an incident.

## What to watch next

- Further OCR enforcement under its risk analysis initiative, which shows no sign of slowing.
- Changes to the HIPAA Security Rule, which may make specific controls mandatory.
- OAIC enforcement in Australia and its expectations for "reasonable steps".

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Conduct and document an organisation-wide information security risk assessment that identifies where health and personal information is stored, and review it at least annually.
- Include health information held by HR, benefits, workers' compensation and other non-clinical functions in the scope of security programmes.
- Maintain written security and privacy policies and procedures, and make sure staff know and follow them.
- Prepare for ransomware with offline backups, segmentation of servers holding sensitive data, and a tested incident response plan.
- Keep evidence of security decisions and controls, as regulators may review them years after an incident.
