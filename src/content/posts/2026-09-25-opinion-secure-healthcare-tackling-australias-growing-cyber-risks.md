## What happened

A Pulse+IT opinion piece warns that Australian healthcare organisations face "a sustained and escalating rise in cyber threats". The author, writing from experience working with healthcare institutions, cites figures showing more than 500 data breaches reported in six months during 2025, affecting an average of over 10,000 individuals. The author notes these reflect only reported cases.

The piece argues healthcare's exposure comes from the combination of high-value data and an uncompromising need for uptime. Patient records give attackers long-lasting identity data. At the same time, reliance on fragmented legacy systems with inconsistent access controls means that "even a minor digital disruption can have immediate and far-reaching consequences" for patient care.

The author also describes a shift in attacker behaviour, from targeting individual applications to exploiting gaps across networks, connected devices and wider digital ecosystems. As a result, security failures increasingly show up as operational disruption rather than IT problems alone.

## Technical and operational significance

The piece identifies three structural risks that, individually, are "well understood" but together create systemic risk that is often underestimated until an incident occurs.

1. **No end-to-end visibility.** EMRs, laboratory platforms, imaging systems and administrative tools often run in silos, so monitoring is fragmented and detection is delayed. When signals cannot be correlated in real time, early indicators such as lateral movement are easily missed.
2. **Inflexible legacy infrastructure.** Many healthcare networks were built for stability and uptime rather than agility. Even when risks are found, rigid architectures, manual processes and multi-vendor dependencies slow the response, keeping organisations in a reactive posture.
3. **Distributed care and connected devices.** As care extends into clinics, remote care and the home, the network perimeter dissolves. Each connected device is a new entry point, and inconsistent policy enforcement across environments increases exposure.

The author stresses that the consequences go beyond technical disruption: downtime delays clinical workflows and diagnostics, reputational damage erodes patient trust, and remediation costs can last long after the incident. "In many cases, the greatest cost is not the breach itself, but the prolonged effort required to restore confidence."

The proposed response is modernisation combined with a shift "from reactive defence to proactive resilience", with resilience "engineered into the digital foundation" rather than added after an incident. The core of this is continuous monitoring across networks, cloud environments and connected devices.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

The argument is written from a vendor perspective, but its diagnosis matches the challenges many Australian hospitals and health networks describe. These include siloed security tooling, clinical systems that cannot be changed quickly, and a growing number of devices and sites outside the traditional hospital network.

The practical value of the piece is in its framing. Security leaders can use it to present cyber risk to executives as a patient-flow and service-continuity issue, not only a data-protection issue. This aligns with the expectations of the Security of Critical Infrastructure regime for hospitals covered by it, and with the ACSC's emphasis on detection and response capability, not just prevention.

## What to watch next

- Notifiable Data Breaches statistics from the OAIC showing whether health sector breach numbers keep rising.
- Investment by health services in centralised security monitoring across clinical, corporate and device networks.
- How state health departments approach security for virtual and home-based care models.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Centralise security logging from EMR, laboratory, imaging, identity and network systems so events can be correlated in one place.
- Prioritise detection of lateral movement, such as unusual internal authentication, remote administration and access between network segments.
- Extend asset management and security policy to clinics, remote care and home-based devices, not just hospital campuses.
- Test incident response through exercises that include clinical leaders and simulate the downtime of key clinical systems, not just IT recovery.
- Include restoring patient and stakeholder trust, through communications and support for affected patients, in breach response planning.
