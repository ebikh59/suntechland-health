## What happened

The practice of Terry J. Dubrow, MD, a plastic surgeon in Beverly Hills, has notified the California Attorney General of a data theft and extortion incident. A person claiming to have breached the practice's network contacted it directly. The investigation found that unauthorised access began on 16 January 2026 and that files were copied.

On 27 July 2026, the practice confirmed that patient information had been obtained, including names, chart information and referring physician details. The data may also have included contact details, Social Security numbers, driver's licence numbers, dates of birth, prescriptions, treatment details, procedure images and X-rays. The number of people affected has not been disclosed, and the extortionist has not been identified.

HIPAA Journal reported four other incidents:

- **SunCloud Health** (Northbrook, Illinois): employee email accounts were accessed between 22 April and 4 May 2026, exposing names and medical information of 2,594 people.
- **Integer Precision Technologies** (Hudson, Massachusetts): a cloud-based file-sharing application was accessed and files were copied, including names, Social Security numbers, driver's licence and passport numbers, financial account details and some health data. It is unclear when the incident was detected.
- **Minnesota ENT** (Oakdale Ear, Nose & Throat): six employee email accounts were accessed. On 15 July 2026, the practice confirmed they contained HIPAA-protected data, including names, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, financial account numbers, and medical and insurance information.
- **Nipro Medical Corp.** (New Jersey): after suspicious IT activity, card data, Social Security numbers and other government IDs may have been viewed or acquired.

## Technical and operational significance

Aesthetic and plastic surgery practices hold unusually sensitive data. Procedure photographs, including before-and-after images, are highly personal, and their exposure can cause serious distress. That makes such practices attractive to extortionists, who may threaten to release images or contact patients directly.

The six-month gap between initial access and confirmation of data theft at the Dubrow practice suggests the intrusion went unnoticed until the attacker made contact.

The other incidents reinforce two recurring entry points: compromised email accounts and cloud file-sharing services.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Australian cosmetic and plastic surgery practices, dermatology clinics and other providers holding clinical photographs face the same risk. Images are often stored on practice management systems, shared drives, cloud storage or clinicians' own devices, sometimes with weaker controls than the main clinical record.

If extortionists contact patients directly, practices need to be ready to respond quickly and compassionately. The ACSC and police can assist, and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme requires timely assessment and notification where serious harm is likely.

## What to watch next

- The number of patients affected at the Dubrow practice and any public release of data.
- Further extortion attacks on aesthetic and specialist practices.
- Counts for the incidents where numbers have not yet been disclosed.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Store clinical photographs and procedure images only in secure, access-controlled clinical systems, not on shared drives, personal phones or consumer cloud services.
- Encrypt image repositories and restrict access to clinicians directly involved in each patient's care.
- Monitor for intrusions continuously so data theft is detected before an extortionist makes contact.
- Prepare an extortion response plan covering engagement with police and the ACSC, patient communication, and support for affected patients.
- Secure email and cloud file-sharing accounts with phishing-resistant MFA and alerts for unusual downloads.
