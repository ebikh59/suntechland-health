## What happened

A new standards body, the Digital Health Standards Council of Australasia (DHSCA), has begun operating. Its aim is to help health services assess the safety and quality of AI and other technologies that are not regulated as medical devices, Pulse+IT reported. The council plans to provide services to technology vendors, clinicians, boards and executives.

According to Healthcare IT News, DHSCA describes itself as a clinically led body that provides assurance and assessment for digital health and AI-enabled healthcare technologies. It plans to seek approval in 2027 to accredit services against national safety and quality standards. It also intends to assess AI tools that fall below the Software as a Medical Device threshold using its six-domain "GUARDS" framework, once that framework has been externally validated.

The council said it is already working with Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations, primary care networks and private hospital groups on AI governance.

## Technical and operational significance

DHSCA is targeting a real gap. TGA regulation applies to software that meets the definition of a medical device, but many AI tools used in healthcare, including documentation aids, administrative automation, patient communication tools and some scribes, fall outside that definition. Health services adopting those tools currently have little independent assurance about their safety, quality or security beyond vendor claims.

An assurance framework for non-regulated tools could help health services, particularly smaller ones without in-house evaluation capability. Its value will depend on:

- **Rigour and independence.** Whether assessments are robust and free of conflicts of interest.
- **Scope.** Whether the framework covers privacy and cyber security as well as clinical safety.
- **Recognition.** Whether regulators, accreditation bodies and purchasers rely on it.

The council's plans are still developing. Its framework has not yet been externally validated, and accreditation approval is not expected until 2027.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Health services should welcome additional sources of assurance for AI tools, but should not treat any single certification as a substitute for their own due diligence. That is particularly true for privacy and security, where obligations under the Privacy Act remain with the health service.

Organisations working with DHSCA, or considering its assessments, should ask how its framework addresses data handling, security controls, model updates and incident management. They should also ask how it aligns with emerging national guidance, such as the ACSQHC's draft AI guidance for health services.

## What to watch next

- Publication and external validation of the GUARDS framework.
- DHSCA's application for accreditation approval in 2027.
- How DHSCA's work fits with TGA regulation, ACSQHC guidance and the government's planned AI safety legislation.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Maintain your own assessment process for non-regulated AI tools, covering clinical safety, privacy, security and vendor practices.
- When using third-party assurance or certifications, review their scope and methodology, especially for privacy and security.
- Require vendors of non-regulated AI tools to provide evidence of testing, data handling practices and incident response arrangements.
- Track national AI guidance and assurance initiatives and update procurement requirements as they mature.
- Involve clinical, privacy, security and consumer representatives in evaluating AI tools before deployment.
