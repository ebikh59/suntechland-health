## What happened

Governance readiness, not speed of deployment, should be the measure of responsible progress on artificial intelligence in health systems, according to a report from the WHO Regional Office for Europe, as reported by Pulse+IT.

The report presents the findings of WHO's first Knowledge Community on responsible AI in health, a five-week structured online dialogue held between October and December 2025 with participants from 105 countries. Its central conclusion is that the main obstacle to responsible AI in health is institutional rather than technical: the strength of the institutions meant to govern AI matters more than the technology itself. WHO/Europe plans to turn the findings into guidance and support for countries.

The report adds to WHO/Europe's wider work on AI readiness. Its 2025 regional readiness report, based on responses from 50 of the region's 53 member states, found that only 8% of countries had liability standards for AI in health. At a July 2026 meeting in Lisbon with 37 countries, WHO/Europe reported that only 8% had a health-specific AI strategy, even though nearly two-thirds already used AI in diagnostics. WHO/Europe has also established a Technical Advisory Group on AI for Health.

## Technical and operational significance

The gap between deployment and governance in these figures is stark. Most countries are already using AI in clinical diagnostics, while very few have the strategies, liability frameworks and oversight structures to manage it.

"Governance readiness" in practice covers several capabilities:

- **Clear accountability** for decisions informed by AI and for harms that result.
- **Evaluation capacity** to assess AI tools before and after deployment.
- **Data governance**, including how patient data is used to train and run AI systems.
- **Monitoring and incident reporting** to detect and learn from failures.
- **Security** of AI systems and the data they rely on.

Each of these depends on people, processes and institutions rather than technology. That supports the report's conclusion that capacity-building, not faster procurement, is what determines whether AI improves care safely.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Although the report focuses on Europe, its message applies in Australia, where AI adoption in health services is accelerating through scribes, decision support and administrative automation. National initiatives such as the ACSQHC's draft AI guidance for health services, TGA reforms and the government's planned AI safety legislation all aim to strengthen governance. Individual health services still need their own capability to put them into practice.

For security and privacy teams, governance readiness includes knowing which AI systems are in use, what data they process, who can access them, and how incidents involving them would be detected and handled.

## What to watch next

- WHO/Europe guidance derived from the Knowledge Community findings.
- Global WHO work on AI liability and governance standards.
- Australian national guidance and legislation on health AI governance.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Assess your organisation's AI governance readiness, covering accountability, evaluation, data governance, monitoring and security, before scaling AI deployments.
- Establish a register of AI systems in use, including the data each processes and its accountable owner.
- Build internal capacity to evaluate AI tools, or arrange access to independent evaluation, rather than relying solely on vendor claims.
- Integrate AI incidents into existing clinical incident and cyber incident reporting processes.
- Align local AI policies with emerging national guidance, such as the ACSQHC's draft AI guidance.
