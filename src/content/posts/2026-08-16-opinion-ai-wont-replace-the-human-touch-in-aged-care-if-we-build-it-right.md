## What happened

A Pulse+IT opinion piece on AI in Australian aged care argues that efficiency and human connection need not compete. It says the biggest threat to connection in aged care homes today is the volume of administration consuming carers' attention.

The piece is based on Regis Aged Care's national rollout of AI with Cognizant. It draws a line between *processing* information and *exercising judgement* on it. AI, the author argues, should "support the people delivering care but not replace their judgement or their authority to act", staying "informative and organisational" so that it does not creep from informing decisions to making them.

Shift handovers were the starting point. At a Regis home, a handover can mean working through more than 200 pages of clinical notes for up to three hours before a shift properly begins. The author notes that none of that time is spent making clinical decisions, only finding the information needed to make them.

Regis set three conditions for the rollout. The AI had to surface every resident and clinical issue with zero tolerance for hallucination. It had to sit safely within the existing systems staff already rely on. And it had to be intuitive enough that time-poor staff would choose to use it rather than work around it.

To test this, AI-generated summaries were benchmarked against a gold standard set by a senior panel of clinicians, across categories such as agitation, medication and infection, and compared on an ongoing basis with a registered nurse baseline. The piece reports that handover reviews fell from up to three hours to less than one hour, that no hallucinations were recorded during the trial, and that note-review accuracy matched or exceeded the registered nurse baseline in most clinical categories.

## Technical and operational significance

The rollout illustrates a sound pattern for introducing generative AI into care settings. Start with a high-burden task where the AI summarises but does not decide. Measure it against clinicians rather than against the vendor's claims. And keep testing after go-live.

Several details matter for anyone attempting something similar:

- **Benchmarking against clinicians, not a generic metric.** Comparing summaries with a clinician-defined gold standard, category by category, provides evidence that clinical governance committees can assess.
- **Integration within existing systems.** Keeping the tool inside systems staff already use reduces workarounds and limits the number of places resident data travels.
- **Trust built over time.** The author stresses transparent testing and honest change management, rather than assuming adoption.

The results are self-reported by an organisation involved in the rollout. They are encouraging but have not been independently evaluated, and the piece notes accuracy matched the nurse baseline in most, not all, categories.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

Aged care providers handle highly sensitive health and personal information. Any AI that reads clinical notes at scale raises privacy and security questions alongside clinical ones: where the data is processed, what the vendor retains, who can access summaries, and how outputs are logged.

The piece does not describe those controls in detail, but they belong in the same governance framework as the clinical testing. Providers considering similar tools should expect to meet obligations under the Privacy Act 1988 and the Aged Care Quality Standards. They should also treat the AI service as a third-party supplier, with security assurance, access controls and incident notification commitments.

## What to watch next

- Independent evaluation of AI-assisted handover tools in aged care, including safety outcomes over longer periods.
- Sector guidance on AI use in aged care from regulators and peak bodies.
- Whether providers extend AI from summarisation into tasks closer to clinical decision-making, and how that boundary is governed.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Before deploying generative AI on clinical records, define clearly which tasks it performs (summarising, sorting) and which remain with clinicians (judgement, decisions).
- Benchmark AI outputs against clinician-defined gold standards across relevant categories, and keep monitoring after go-live rather than relying on a one-off pilot.
- Complete a privacy impact assessment covering where resident data is processed, what the vendor stores or uses for training, and how long outputs are retained.
- Keep AI tools inside existing, access-controlled clinical systems where possible, and log who views generated summaries.
- Include AI suppliers in third-party risk management, with contractual security requirements and breach notification obligations.
