## What happened

A Pulse+IT opinion piece argues that the biggest obstacle to AI in Australian healthcare is not the technology but the data beneath it. The author writes that AI could automate or augment up to half the tasks across the health system, but only if the underlying data is good quality, accessible and trustworthy. Today, much of it "remains fragmented, poorly governed, and locked in silos".

Without a trusted data foundation, the piece says, AI simply magnifies existing limitations, which is why so many healthcare AI initiatives stall at the pilot stage.

The author points to the 2022 Medibank breach as a reminder of what is at stake, describing it as one of the largest data breaches in Australian history and something that continues to shape how people think about sharing health data. Many Australians remain reluctant to share health information without clear safeguards and informed consent.

The piece cites the Hong Kong Hospital Authority, responsible for more than nine million patient records across 43 public hospitals and 30 source systems, as an example of building the data foundation first. According to the author, real-time visibility into patient flow helped reduce A&E access block from around 12% to 3%, and the same platform now supports AI-driven diagnosis and treatment initiatives.

It also cites Cloudera's 2026 Data Readiness Index, which found nearly four in five organisations say their AI initiatives are limited by restricted access to data across environments. Only 27% of APAC organisations reported that their data sources are fully integrated.

## Technical and operational significance

The central argument is about sequence and architecture. Rather than adding privacy, security and consent as controls on top of data, the author argues they should be embedded in how data is managed, shared and used from the outset. "Governance follows the data, wherever it resides."

The piece also rejects consolidating everything into one environment. Health data is spread across electronic medical records, pathology systems, administration platforms, and private, public and on-premises infrastructure. In NSW alone, the author notes, patient information sits across multiple systems without a current statewide connection. Moving large volumes of sensitive data "adds cost and risk without solving the underlying problem". The alternative proposed is to bring AI to the data where it already resides.

The piece is written from a data platform vendor's perspective, which readers should keep in mind. Its core point is still widely shared: AI governance is mostly data governance.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

For security and privacy teams, the argument has practical consequences. Every new copy of patient data created to feed an AI project is another store to secure, monitor and include in breach response. Approaches that keep data in place and control access centrally can reduce that exposure, provided access controls, logging and consent rules are actually applied consistently across environments.

The Medibank reference is a reminder that public trust is fragile. Australian health organisations planning AI programmes need to show patients and regulators, under the Privacy Act and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, that data used for AI is governed as carefully as data used for care.

## What to watch next

- Progress on statewide and national health data integration, including in NSW.
- Privacy Act reforms and how they affect secondary use of health data for AI.
- Evidence from Australian health services that data-first approaches move AI projects beyond the pilot stage.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Map where patient data used or proposed for AI projects resides, who can access it and under what consent or legal basis, before selecting AI tools.
- Avoid creating new uncontrolled copies of patient data for AI work; prefer approaches that keep data in governed environments with central access control.
- Apply consistent logging and access monitoring across EMR, pathology, cloud and on-premises data stores used for analytics or AI.
- Build privacy impact assessments and consent checks into the start of AI projects rather than the end.
- Include AI data pipelines and any derived datasets in breach response planning and Notifiable Data Breaches assessments.
