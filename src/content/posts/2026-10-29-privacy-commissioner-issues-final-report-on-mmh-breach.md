## What happened

New Zealand Privacy Commissioner Michael Webster has released his second and final report into the December 2025 cyber breach of the Manage My Health patient portal. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner published the Phase 2 report on 27 October 2026, describing it as a wake-up call for agencies.

According to Pulse+IT, Mr Webster warned that the breach has significantly damaged public trust in digital health services, with some patients now requesting paper records rather than using online portals.

Reporting on the report says the Commissioner found Manage My Health breached privacy rules by:

- collecting some hospital data without consent,
- keeping information for longer than necessary, and
- failing to manage inactive accounts.

Among the recommendations reported by MLex, the Commissioner proposed deleting registered but never-activated accounts after three months and contacting dormant account holders after 12 months. He also recommended that Manage My Health and Health NZ work to rebuild trust in Northland's health services and patient portals, since most affected patients live in Northland. The OPC said it had received around 50 complaints, which it will handle through its usual dispute resolution processes.

The final report follows the Phase 1 findings that both organisations breached the security rule of the Health Information Privacy Code, and the compliance notices issued in September.

## Technical and operational significance

The Phase 2 findings move from "how did the attackers get in" to "why was there so much data to steal". Data minimisation, retention and account lifecycle management are often treated as administrative matters. In a breach, they determine how many people are affected and how much of their information is exposed.

Never-activated and dormant accounts are a clear example. Each holds patient information and possibly credentials, but provides no benefit to the patient. Deleting or disabling them reduces both the volume of data at risk and the number of accounts attackers could take over.

Collecting hospital data without consent points to another common problem: integration feeds that send more data to a third-party platform than patients expect or than the platform needs.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

The trust impact is the most important lesson for Australia. Digital health strategies depend on patients being willing to use portals, apps and shared records. A breach that pushes patients back to paper undermines years of investment and can make care less coordinated and less safe.

Australian Privacy Principle 11.2 requires organisations to destroy or de-identify personal information they no longer need. Australian health services and portal providers should check whether inactive accounts, legacy records and integration feeds comply in practice. They should also make sure patients understand what data flows into third-party platforms.

## What to watch next

- Implementation of the recommendations on inactive accounts and data retention.
- Measures by Health NZ and Manage My Health to rebuild trust in Northland.
- Whether patient portal use in New Zealand recovers, and what lessons Australian regulators draw.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- Set and enforce retention periods for patient portal data, deleting or de-identifying information that is no longer needed.
- Disable or delete never-activated accounts after a short period, and contact dormant account holders before closing their accounts.
- Review integration feeds to third-party platforms so that only data patients would reasonably expect, and that the service needs, is shared.
- Explain clearly to patients what information appears in portals and apps and where it comes from.
- Include a trust-recovery plan, covering communication, support and paper alternatives, in breach response planning for patient-facing services.
