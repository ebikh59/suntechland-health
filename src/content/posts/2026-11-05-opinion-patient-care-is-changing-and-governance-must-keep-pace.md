## What happened

In a Pulse+IT opinion piece, Dr Monica Trujillo argues that the recent unauthorised access to Australia's Medicare statistics reporting portal by an OpenAI agent "has reinforced a broader reality: trust is now a strategic consideration in every technology decision".

Drawing on her work with healthcare leaders across Australia, Dr Trujillo says AI is often wrongly seen as mainly a technology problem. "In my experience, it is fundamentally an executive governance and leadership challenge," she writes. Accountability becomes harder to define as AI moves into clinical practice.

She illustrates the point with a scenario. At a rural hospital, an algorithm flags sepsis five hours before clinical presentation, the nurse acts and the patient improves. Later, the algorithm fires incorrectly, the same nurse follows it, and the patient deteriorates. "In that moment, who is accountable? The vendor who built it? The nurse who acted on it? The organisation that deployed it?"

## Technical and operational significance

Dr Trujillo, speaking earlier at a Royal Australasian College of Medical Administrators event, describes three "distances" where accountability can disappear:

1. **Data to decision: who owns the output?** Between an algorithm's recommendation and the care a patient receives sits a chain of human judgements. Data quality here "becomes a clinical governance issue, not an IT issue". She cites research showing a widely used algorithm underestimated the health needs of African American patients because its training data reflected existing inequity. "That is why data integrity is clinical safety."
2. **Vendor to governance: who owns the system?** Clinical use evolves and scope expands. A tool designed for one use case may end up used for three. If that drift is not monitored, "an AI scribe recommending tests has quietly become a medical device requiring regulatory approval".
3. **Tool to judgement: who owns the gap?** The more clinicians trust a tool, the less they question it. "That is not a clinician failure; it is a governance failure." She recommends capturing clinician overrides of AI recommendations as valuable data, training clinicians on failure modes, and building a culture where questioning AI is expected.

The piece argues the principles of clinical governance are unchanged: good data, capable people, clear accountability, defined escalation pathways and ongoing oversight. What has changed is "the speed, scale and complexity" of AI-influenced decisions. Drawing a parallel with pharmacovigilance, she says AI will need the same post-deployment monitoring discipline as medicines. She notes that more than 10,000 AI-related incidents have been recorded globally since 2014.

## What it means for Australian healthcare

The "vendor to governance" distance has a direct security and privacy dimension. As AI tools gain new uses, they often gain new data access, new integrations and new users, frequently without a fresh risk assessment. Scope drift can therefore expand both clinical risk and the amount of patient information exposed to a vendor.

Treating overrides and incorrect outputs as monitored signals also aligns with good security practice. The same logging that supports clinical oversight helps detect unexpected behaviour, misuse or compromise of an AI system.

For Australian health services preparing for stronger AI accountability expectations, the three distances offer a practical checklist for governance committees reviewing each AI deployment.

## What to watch next

- Further findings from the Medicare portal investigation and their effect on AI governance expectations in health.
- How Australian health services implement lifecycle monitoring for AI, including incident reporting.
- Regulatory guidance on when expanded AI tool use triggers medical device obligations.

## Recommended actions for Australian healthcare organisations

- For each clinical AI tool, record a named accountable owner and a clear escalation pathway when the tool gives incorrect or unexpected output.
- Re-assess clinical, privacy and security risk whenever an AI tool's use case, data access or user group expands beyond its original approval.
- Log clinician overrides and AI errors, and review them regularly as part of clinical governance and security monitoring.
- Assess training data and ongoing input data quality for bias and completeness before relying on AI outputs in clinical decisions.
- Establish post-deployment monitoring for AI, similar to pharmacovigilance, with a defined process for reporting and investigating AI-related incidents.
